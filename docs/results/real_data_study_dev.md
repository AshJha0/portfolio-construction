# Portfolio-construction levers on real data: development period

**Development data only (1926-07-01 to 2015-12-31).** No row dated 2016-01-01 or later was read: the script loads `load_returns(..., period="development")` and nothing else. The series are research index portfolios from the Kenneth R. French Data Library; they cannot be bought, the trading costs are assumptions, and nothing here is investment advice.

Generated by `python python/studies/real_data_study.py --phase dev`. Every number is in `real_data_study_dev.csv` (one row per configuration and period; summary statistics only, no return series). Rerunning on the same data vintage reproduces both files.

## What the development data say

Plain answers first; the tables below carry the intervals. Differences are in annualized net Sharpe ratio at 10 bp, with two-sided p-values of the paired Ledoit-Wolf test; with about 88 years of daily data on 10 industries the standard error of one Sharpe ratio is about 0.12 (bootstrap), and that of a paired difference between two similar rules is far smaller.

1. **Nothing beat 1/N by much, and the market was the weakest benchmark.** 1/N on 10 industries earned 0.47 [0.24, 0.71] net of cost; the market 0.41. Most construction rules land within 0.05 of 1/N.
2. **Volatility targeting gave the largest gain on 10 industries, but not a steady one.** 1/N scaled to 10% volatility (sample covariance, 252 days, gross at most 1.5) earned 0.60, 0.13 (p = 0.02) against plain 1/N, and its maximum drawdown was 44% against 82%. The gain comes almost entirely from 1928-1969 (the 1930s); in 1970-2015 it is near zero or negative (sub-period table). On 49 industries, which start in 1974, it is 0.03 (p = 0.58). This is the pattern the critiques of volatility management describe (Cederburg et al. 2020): real, but concentrated in a few episodes.
3. **Minimum variance lowered volatility by about a third and added a little Sharpe.** On 49 industries long-only minimum variance (LW constant correlation, 504 days) earned 0.63 against 1/N 0.49, 0.15 (p = 0.15), at 11.2% volatility against 16.4%; on 10 industries the gain is 0.03 (p = 0.56). In both universes the gain is in 2000-2015; in the earlier sub-periods minimum variance is slightly behind 1/N.
4. **The covariance estimator hardly matters for long-only rules, except that EWMA hurts.** Every linear or nonlinear shrinkage estimator and the PCA model are within 0.02 of the sample covariance for both minimum variance and ERC; the long-only constraint already does most of the shrinking (Jagannathan and Ma 2003). EWMA with decay 0.97 trades five times as much and loses: on 49 industries -0.13 (p = 0.03) against the sample covariance.
5. **Cost-aware rebalancing saves cost but the cost was small to begin with.** At 10 bp the long-only rules pay 0.03 to 0.23% a year; bands and partial rebalancing cut that by up to half and move Sharpe ratios by at most about 0.02 (minimum variance on 10 industries, partial 0.5: 0.01 (p = 0.02)). Turnover-penalised minimum variance cuts turnover by 75 to 95% against full rebalancing, with no reliable Sharpe change: `min_var_lo_tc.lwcc` L252 gamma10: 0.02 (p = 0.31); `min_var_lo_tc.lwcc` L252 gamma2: 0.02 (p = 0.65); `min_var_lo_tc.lwcc` L504 gamma10: 0.00 (p = 0.90); `min_var_lo_tc.lwcc` L504 gamma2: 0.01 (p = 0.88).
6. **Leverage control matters for the only rule that needs it.** The uncapped tangency portfolio of trailing sample moments (the library rule now passes no leverage guard) lost all of its capital between February and May 1929 at both windows and at both costs, so it appears in no table (see Fallbacks and exclusions). Capping gross leverage at 2 by moving toward minimum variance kept it solvent: 504 days 0.59, 0.13 (p = 0.10) against 1/N, with turnover 5.1 times NAV a year and a 69% drawdown; it loses 0.06 of Sharpe between 10 and 25 bp.
7. **Bayes-Stein means did not help the max-Sharpe rule.** 0.00 (p = 0.87) against sample means; both trade about 5 to 6 times NAV a year and lose 0.04 to 0.06 of Sharpe between 10 and 25 bp.
8. **The deflated Sharpe ratio does not separate the rules.** With N = 206 registered trials most configurations have DSR near 1 because 88 years of data make even a Sharpe ratio of 0.45 clearly positive; DSR tests the Sharpe ratio against zero after selection, not one rule against another. The paired tests are the relevant evidence, and few are below 0.05 before any correction for the comparisons made.

## Method

**Universes and periods.** `ind10` is the 10 value-weighted industry portfolios from 1926-07-01; `ind49` is the 49 industry portfolios from 1972-01-03 (the file has missing values up to 1971-03-11). Each universe is evaluated from the first calendar month that has at least 504 trading days of history before it (1928-04-02 and 1974-02-01), to 2015-12-31. Every configuration of a universe is evaluated on exactly the same days, so paired tests compare like with like.

**Schedule and accounting.** Rebalancing is monthly, on the first trading day of each calendar month, with weights computed from returns up to the previous close (`walkforward.calendar_rebalance_rows`). The accounting is `portopt.research.walkforward.run_schedule_net`: weights drift between rebalances, each rebalance (including the initial build from cash) pays `cost_bps` on the traded risky notional `sum_i |w_new_i - w_drift_i|`, cash earns the daily T-bill rate `RF` from `ff3_daily`, and negative cash borrows at the same rate. Short positions earn the negative of the asset return and pay no borrow fee. Every rule sees only the trailing window before the rebalance.

**Costs are assumptions.** The industry portfolios are index series; nobody trades them, so there is no cost to measure. 10 bp per unit of one-way traded notional stands in for trading a basket of large US stocks that tracks an industry; 25 bp is a stress level. Neither is calibrated to any desk, and there is no market impact term.

**Estimation windows.** 252 and 504 trading days (about one and two years). The two values bracket common practice and show sensitivity; we did not search over windows. The concentration `N / T` is 0.04 and 0.02 for 10 industries and 0.19 and 0.10 for 49; by the Marchenko-Pastur law (derived) the smallest eigenvalue of a sample covariance of uncorrelated, equal-variance assets is biased down to about `(1 - sqrt(N/T))^2` of the truth: 0.64 for 10 industries at 252 days and 0.31 for 49 industries at 252 days. That bias is what shrinkage estimators target.

**Levers and their bases.** Each lever is judged against its own base configuration ("vs base") as well as against 1/N and the market:

| Lever | Configurations | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Covariance estimators | long-only minimum variance and ERC with the sample covariance, Ledoit-Wolf constant correlation (core `shrink_covariance`), Ledoit-Wolf toward the identity, Ledoit-Wolf (2020) nonlinear shrinkage, EWMA with decay 0.97 (half-life 22.8 days; the RiskMetrics one-month decay), and a 3-factor PCA model | the sample covariance |
| Cost-aware rebalancing | 1/N, minimum variance (LW constant correlation) and, on 10 industries, long-only max-Sharpe, each with a no-trade band of 0.05 or 0.10 on the L1 distance to target, or closing half the gap (partial 0.5); on 10 industries also the turnover-penalised minimum variance `portopt.min_variance_turnover` with `w0` the drifted weights and penalty `kappa = 12 c / gamma`, gamma 2 or 10 | the same rule rebalanced in full |
| Leverage control (10 industries) | tangency portfolio of the trailing sample moments (excess returns), uncapped, and with gross leverage capped at 2 by moving toward minimum variance (`tangency_capped`) | uncapped tangency |
| Volatility targeting | 1/N and minimum variance (LW constant correlation) scaled each month to a 10% ex-ante volatility with the sample covariance or the EWMA covariance of the window, gross exposure capped at 1.5 | the unscaled rule |
| Mean shrinkage (10 industries) | long-only max-Sharpe (25-point frontier grid) with Bayes-Stein (Jorion 1986) or sample means of excess returns | sample means |

The 10% target and the 1.5 cap were fixed before any result was seen. A Sharpe ratio is invariant to a constant scale, so the target matters only through the cap and the cash it leaves. Rules that use means (max-Sharpe, tangency) see excess returns; covariance-only rules see total returns, as the forward runner does.

**Statistics.** Sharpe ratios are annualized `sqrt(252) mean / std` of daily net excess returns (net return minus `RF`). Standard errors: Lo (2002) iid and Mertens (2002) iid non-normal. The 95% interval is a stationary bootstrap (Politis and Romano 1994), mean block 63 days, 1000 resamples, seed 42, computed at 10 bp. PSR is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds 0 (Bailey and Lopez de Prado 2012); DSR raises the hurdle to the expected maximum of 206 trials (Bailey and Lopez de Prado 2014). "dSR (p)" is the difference in annualized Sharpe ratio and the two-sided p-value of the Ledoit and Wolf (2008) HAC test (`sharpe_difference_test`, Bartlett kernel, 13 lags on these lengths). Sub-periods are slices of the same runs.

**Trial count.** Every configuration on this page was registered before the run that produced it: 206 configurations in `data/real/registry.json` under `r2-dev-exploration` (198, registered 2026-10-31T15:14:01Z, configurations SHA-256 `6065911e5965d19d`) and `r2-dev-exploration-tc` (8, the turnover-penalised batch, added once that optimiser was ready). The DSR uses N = 206 and the cross-sectional variance of the per-period Sharpe ratios of the universe's configurations. Counting cost levels and near-duplicates (EWMA at 252 and 504 days is nearly the same estimator) as separate trials overstates the number of independent trials, so the DSR is conservative.

**Block length and serial dependence.** Squared daily returns of 1/N on 10 industries have autocorrelation 0.24 at lag 1 and 0.10 at lag 21 (volatility clustering). The bootstrap standard error of 1/N's Sharpe ratio is 0.100 with block 1 (iid), 0.118 with 21, 0.123 with 63 and 0.131 with 126 days, against an iid Lo standard error of 0.104 (measured while choosing the block length). The iid standard errors, PSR and DSR therefore overstate precision by roughly 20%.

**Annualization.** The NYSE traded on Saturdays until 1952-05-24, so 1926-1952 has about 290 rows a year. We annualize with 252 throughout (the library convention), which understates annualized returns and costs in those years by about 13% and Sharpe ratios by about 7%. Comparisons between rules on the same days keep their sign and test statistics.

**Tooling checks run with this page.** The scaled calendar engine used for volatility targeting reproduced `run_schedule_net` and `run_backtest_net` bit for bit in 6 checks. Long-only minimum variance is solved by the primal active-set method in `python/studies/real_data_engine.py`, because the pinned core QP is written for n <= 20 with explicit loops and takes seconds a solve at n = 49 (measured; the turnover-penalised split QP took about 600 s for one solve at n = 49, which is why that lever is run on 10 industries only). Every solve carries a KKT certificate, and on 360 dates compared with `portopt.min_variance_longonly` the largest weight difference is 2.1e-15.

## Limits

* The series are not investable. Industry portfolios are value-weighted baskets of every CRSP stock in an industry, rebalanced by construction, with no trading cost of their own.
* Costs are assumed, linear and the same in 1930 and 2015. Real costs were far higher before the 1980s, which would penalize the higher-turnover rules more in the early decades.
* One data vintage. The French library rebuilds its history monthly; the numbers here are for vintage 1 (CRSP 202609).
* The development period was not blind: these rules and estimators were published by people who had seen much of this history. The holdout (2016 onward) is clean only with respect to this repository's own choices, which the registry makes countable.
* Standard errors that assume independence understate the uncertainty by about 20% (block bootstrap comparison above).
