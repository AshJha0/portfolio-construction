# LEARN: portfolio construction from Markowitz to risk parity

This document teaches the theory implemented in `portopt`: mean-variance
optimisation and its closed forms, why the raw recipe fails on estimated
inputs, covariance shrinkage, constrained optimisation and its KKT
conditions, Black-Litterman, risk parity, and the backtest with its costs
and error bars. It is written for two readers at once: a university
student meeting the subject for the first time, and a reviewer at a fund
who wants to check every number.

Each section opens with the plain idea, then states it formally, adds a
short glossary note where a term needs one, and ends with a worked example
whose numbers come from the repository.

## How to read the numbers

**The data is synthetic.** The bundled universe (`US_EQ, EU_EQ, JP_EQ,
UK_EQ, EM_EQ, US_BOND, IG_CORP, FX_CARRY`; 1250 daily returns) is
generated by `data/generate_data.py` with seed 42: three Gaussian factors
(global equity, rates, carry) plus Student-t(6) idiosyncratic noise,
independent across days, with constant drifts and fixed market caps. The
asset names are labels, not market data. No result in this document is
evidence about real markets. The upside of synthetic data is that we know
the true parameters, so we can measure how far the estimates miss them
(section 2).

Every factual claim carries one of four evidence labels:

* **[derived]**: follows from the algebra shown, or from reading the code
  at the path given.
* **[measured: ...]**: produced by running code. `golden <case>` means the
  value is stored in `data/golden/golden.json` (written by
  `data/generate_data.py`, checked by all four test suites); `demo` means
  the output of `cd python && PYTHONPATH=src python demo.py`; `COOKBOOK n`
  means recipe n in [COOKBOOK.md](COOKBOOK.md), which reproduces the number.
* **[cited: ...]**: taken from the named reference (section 10).
* **[assumed]**: a modelling choice or convention, not a fact.

Unless stated otherwise, moments are annualised with factor 252 and the
risk-free rate is 2% (`data/config.json`).

## 1. Mean-variance foundations

### 1.1 The utility view

**Plain idea.** A portfolio is a list of weights. We judge it by two
numbers: how much it is expected to earn and how much it wobbles. A more
cautious investor gives up more expected return to reduce the wobble.

**Formal statement.** With weights `w`, expected returns `mu` and
covariance `Sigma`, the portfolio has mean `mu'w` and variance
`w' Sigma w` (Markowitz 1952) [cited: Markowitz 1952]. The standard
objective is quadratic utility, which is also exact for normal returns
under exponential (CARA) utility [cited: Merton 1972; derived]:

```
U(w) = mu'w - (delta/2) * w' Sigma w
dU/dw = mu - delta * Sigma w = 0   =>   w* = (1/delta) * Sigma^{-1} mu
```

**Glossary.** `delta` is risk aversion: the expected return the investor
demands per unit of variance. `Sigma^{-1}` is the inverse covariance (the
precision matrix).

Most of this project is a variation on `Sigma^{-1} mu` [derived]:
Markowitz computes it, Black-Litterman runs it backwards, shrinkage
repairs the `Sigma` that goes into it, and risk parity drops `mu`.

There are two ways to read the optimum. In the trade-off reading, each
weight is an expected return scaled down by risk and by correlation with
the other assets. In the hedging reading, `Sigma^{-1}` combines assets so
that each position is partly a bet and partly a hedge of the other bets.
The second reading explains why optimal weights can be large and long one
asset and short a highly correlated sibling (section 2).

**Worked example.** Section 5.1 runs this formula backwards on the
bundled data: with `delta = 2.5` the market weights imply EM_EQ earns
3.85% [measured: golden `implied_pi`, `pi_4`].

### 1.2 The efficient frontier and the two-fund theorem

**Plain idea.** For every target return there is a cheapest way, in
variance, to reach it. The set of those cheapest portfolios is the
efficient frontier. Without position limits, every point on it is a mix
of just two fixed portfolios.

**Formal statement.** Minimise `(1/2) w' Sigma w` subject to `mu'w = m`
and `1'w = 1`. The Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}(w,\lambda,\gamma)
= \tfrac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w
- \lambda\,(\mu^\top w - m) - \gamma\,(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1),
\qquad
\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = \Sigma w - \lambda \mu - \gamma \mathbf{1} = 0,
$$

so `w = Sigma^{-1}(lam*mu + gam*1)`. Substituting into the two
constraints leaves a 2x2 system [derived]:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} B & A \\ A & C \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} m \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det = BC - A^2 = D .
$$

Its coefficients are the four scalars the code calls A, B, C, D
(`two_fund_scalars` in `python/src/portopt/markowitz.py`):

```
A = 1' Sigma^{-1} mu     B = mu' Sigma^{-1} mu
C = 1' Sigma^{-1} 1      D = B*C - A^2
```

`B > 0` and `C > 0` because `Sigma^{-1}` is positive definite, and
Cauchy-Schwarz gives `D >= 0`, with equality only when `mu` is
proportional to `1` [derived]. In that case the frontier is a single
point and the code raises a "degenerate frontier" error (it tests
`D <= 1e-12 * B * C`) [derived: `_two_fund_gh`]. Solving for the
multipliers gives the frontier in closed form, with `x = Sigma^{-1} mu`
and `y = Sigma^{-1} 1`:

```
w(m) = g + h*m,   g = (B*y - A*x)/D,   h = (C*x - A*y)/D
var(m) = (C*m^2 - 2*A*m + B) / D
```

This is the two-fund theorem: frontier weights are affine in the target
[derived]. The variance is a parabola in `m` whose vertex is the global
minimum-variance (GMV) portfolio

```
w_mv = Sigma^{-1} 1 / C,   return A/C,   variance 1/C
```

`w_mv` does not involve `mu`. Section 2 explains why that makes it the
most stable point on the frontier.

**Glossary.** *Frontier*: the minimum variance attainable at each target
return. *GMV*: the frontier portfolio with the lowest variance overall.

**Worked example** [measured: golden `two_fund_scalars_annualized`,
`minvar_unconstrained`, `frontier_point_t080`]:

```
A = 20.1810855643   B = 1.3427105101
C = 626.0618354614  D = B*C - A^2 = 433.3435918688
```

* GMV return `A/C = 20.1810855643 / 626.0618354614 = 0.0322349717`
  (3.22% a year) and volatility `sqrt(1/C) = 0.0399660645` (4.00%),
  matching `minvar_unconstrained.ret` and `.vol`. The GMV weights hold
  70.1% in US_BOND and are short EM_EQ by 4.6%.
* Frontier variance at `m = 0.08`:
  `(626.0618*0.0064 - 2*20.1810856*0.08 + 1.3427105) / 433.3435919
  = 2.1205325 / 433.3435919 = 0.0048934208`, matching
  `frontier_point_t080.variance` (volatility 7.0%).

### 1.3 Adding a risk-free asset: the tangency portfolio

**Plain idea.** If cash pays a fixed rate, any mix of cash and one risky
portfolio lies on a straight line in (volatility, return) space. The
steepest such line gives the best return per unit of risk. Its risky
portfolio is the tangency portfolio.

**Formal statement** [derived: `tangency_weights`]:

```
w_tan = Sigma^{-1}(mu - rf*1) / (A - rf*C)
sharpe_tan = sqrt(B - 2*rf*A + rf^2*C)
```

The denominator `A - rf*C = 1' Sigma^{-1}(mu - rf*1)` must be positive,
which means `rf < A/C`: the risk-free rate must be below the GMV return
[derived]. Otherwise no line from `(0, rf)` touches the upper frontier
and the code raises `TangencyUndefinedError`. The code also raises when
`A - rf*C <= 1e-6 * max(|A|, |rf|*C)`, because the weights scale like
`1/(A - rf*C)` and reach gross leverage of a million or more there
[derived: `TANGENCY_COND_RTOL` in `markowitz.py`].

With a risk-free asset the two-fund theorem becomes a one-fund theorem:
every mean-variance investor holds cash plus the same tangency portfolio,
in proportions set by risk aversion [derived]. Black-Litterman uses this
logic in reverse (section 5).

**Glossary.** *Sharpe ratio*: excess return over the risk-free rate,
divided by volatility. *Gross leverage*: `sum_i |w_i|`, which is 1 for
any long-only portfolio.

**Worked example** [measured: golden `tangency_unconstrained`]:
`sqrt(1.3427105 - 0.04*20.1810856 + 0.0004*626.0618355)
= sqrt(0.7858918) = 0.8865054`, matching `tangency_unconstrained.sharpe`
at rf = 0.02. Gross leverage is 4.14. In the backtest the error fires at
8 of 12 rebalance dates, wherever the expanding-window GMV return falls
below 2% [measured: COOKBOOK 18].

## 2. Why raw Markowitz fails: estimation error maximisation

**Plain idea.** We never know `mu` and `Sigma`; we estimate them. The
optimiser treats the estimates as truth and leans hardest on whatever
looks best, and what looks best is often what was overestimated by
chance.

**Formal statement.** With T observations of N assets:

* the sample mean of an asset with 16% annual volatility over 5 years has
  standard error `0.16 / sqrt(5) = 7.2%` a year [derived], which is as
  large as any plausible equity risk premium;
* the sample covariance has `N(N+1)/2` free parameters; for N large
  relative to T its smallest eigenvalues are biased downward, and it is
  singular for `T <= N` [cited: Ledoit and Wolf 2004].

`w* ~ Sigma^{-1} mu` amplifies errors along directions where `Sigma` has
small eigenvalues, because `Sigma^{-1}` magnifies exactly those directions
[derived]. A small spurious return difference between two highly
correlated assets then becomes a large long-short position. Michaud
(1989) called this error maximisation [cited: Michaud 1989]. Chopra and
Ziemba (1993) found errors in means cost roughly ten times as much as
errors in variances [cited: Chopra and Ziemba 1993].

**Glossary.** *Standard error*: the standard deviation of an estimate
across repeated samples. *Error maximisation*: the tendency of an
optimiser to overweight the inputs with the largest positive estimation
error.

**Worked example.** Because the data is synthetic we can compare the
estimates with the truth set in `data/generate_data.py` (`MU_ANNUAL`):

| Asset | True drift [derived: generator] | Sample mean [measured: demo] |
|---|---|---|
| US_EQ | 7.0% | 9.4% |
| EM_EQ | 9.0% | 2.9% |

EM_EQ has the highest true drift and one of the lowest sample means. Its
sample volatility is 20.3% [measured: demo], so the standard error of a
5-year mean is about `0.203 / sqrt(4.96) = 9.1%` [derived], and a miss of
6.1 points is unremarkable. The optimiser acts on the estimates anyway:

* `tangency_unconstrained` holds +135.6% US_EQ, -57.6% EU_EQ, -41.2%
  UK_EQ and -38.3% EM_EQ [measured: golden `tangency_unconstrained`].
  The sample correlation of US_EQ and EU_EQ is 0.82 [measured: COOKBOOK
  1], so the optimiser is betting on a small, noise-dominated difference
  in their means, and it is short the asset whose true drift is highest.
* Shifting US_EQ's mean by one percentage point moves the unconstrained
  tangency weights by 0.58 of NAV in L1 distance; the same shift moves
  the long-only frontier point at 6% by 0.13 [measured: COOKBOOK 13].

The remedies in this library attack different parts of the pipeline:

| Remedy | Attacks | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Shrink the covariance | noise in `Sigma` | 3 |
| Constrain the weights | noise amplification | 4 |
| Replace `mu` with equilibrium plus views | noise in `mu` | 5 |
| Drop `mu` entirely | noise in `mu` | 6 |

## 3. Covariance shrinkage (Ledoit-Wolf)

### 3.1 The idea

**Plain idea.** The sample covariance is unbiased but noisy. A simple
structured guess, "every pair of assets has the same correlation", is
biased but has almost no noise. A weighted average of the two can beat
both, and the right weight can be estimated from the data.

**Formal statement.** With sample covariance `S` and constant-correlation
target `F` (every pair has correlation `rbar`, the average sample
correlation):

```
Sigma_shrunk = delta * F + (1 - delta) * S,    delta in [0, 1]
delta* = max(0, min(1, (pi_hat - rho_hat) / (T * gamma_hat)))
```

Ledoit and Wolf (2004) derive the `delta` that minimises expected
Frobenius loss and estimate it from the data [cited: Ledoit and Wolf
2004]. The three ingredients are:

* `pi_hat`: total sampling variance of the entries of `S`, the sum over
  (i, j) of `(1/T) sum_t (x_ti x_tj - S_ij)^2`;
* `rho_hat`: a correction for the covariance between the noise in `S`
  and the noise in `F`, which is built from the same data;
* `gamma_hat`: the squared distance `sum_ij (F_ij - S_ij)^2` between
  target and sample.

Much noise and little distance gives a large `delta`; the reverse gives a
small one [derived]. The code sets `delta = 0` when `gamma_hat <= 0`
[derived: `shrink_covariance` in `estimators.py`]. When `delta > 0` and
`F` is positive definite, the shrunk matrix is positive definite even if
`S` is singular [derived].

**Glossary.** *Frobenius loss*: the sum of squared entry-wise errors.
*Shrinkage intensity*: the weight `delta` on the target.

### 3.2 The pinned estimator and a worked number

`portopt` implements the constant-correlation estimator as in Ledoit and
Wolf (2004), Appendix B, which is the formula in the authors' `covCor.m`
[cited: Ledoit and Wolf 2004; derived: `estimators.py`]. Two details
matter and are stated in API_SPEC section 2.2:

* `pi_hat`, `rho_hat`, `gamma_hat` use the paper's divisor-T covariance.
  The shrunk matrix is returned in the project's ddof=1 convention, which
  is a pure `T/(T-1)` rescaling; `rbar` and `delta` are scale-free
  [derived].
* The off-diagonal part of `rho_hat` is
  `sum_{i != j} (rbar/2) [sqrt(s_jj/s_ii) theta_ii,ij + sqrt(s_ii/s_jj) theta_jj,ij]`.
  Summed over ordered pairs, that puts weight `rbar` (not `rbar/2`) on
  `theta_ii,ij`. Keeping `rbar/2` per ordered pair halves the correction
  and over-shrinks; on this data it gives 0.00943 instead of 0.00721
  [measured: recorded in the generator's validation history, not
  re-run here]. The generator refuses to write golden values unless the
  intensity matches a direct transcription of `covCor.m` to 1e-14
  [derived: `data/generate_data.py`].

**Worked example** [measured: golden `shrinkage_const_corr_daily`]:

```
rbar  = 0.4171937171   (average pairwise correlation)
delta = 0.0072117652   (intensity)
```

Diagonals are unchanged by construction (`F_ii = S_ii`), so
`shrunk_0_0 = cov_0_0 = 9.354067081e-05`. The off-diagonal `S_01 =
7.746918e-05` moves to `7.719553e-05`. The intensity is under 1% because
T = 1250 is large relative to N = 8 [derived]. On shorter windows of the
same panel it grows [measured: COOKBOOK 14]:

| T (first T rows) | 10 | 20 | 60 | 120 | 250 | 500 | 1250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| delta | 0.439 | 0.257 | 0.162 | 0.074 | 0.037 | 0.018 | 0.007 |

On the first five rows (T = 5 < N = 8) the sample covariance is singular,
`delta = 0.347`, and the shrunk matrix passes the Cholesky gate
[measured: COOKBOOK 14]. For N = 2 the target equals the sample
covariance, so `delta = 0` [derived]. Shrinkage improves out-of-sample
minimum-variance portfolios in the published evidence [cited: Ledoit and
Wolf 2004]; it does nothing for expected returns [derived].

## 4. Constrained optimisation: why long-only changes everything

### 4.1 No more closed form

**Plain idea.** Once weights may not go below zero (or above a cap), we
no longer know in advance which limits will bind. The solver has to find
that set.

**Formal statement.** The constrained problems in `portopt` are

```
minimise    (1/2) w' Sigma w
subject to  1'w = 1,  [mu'w = m],  lb <= w <= ub
```

a strictly convex quadratic programme (QP) when `Sigma` is positive
definite, so the minimiser is unique [derived]. With box bounds each
coordinate can sit at its lower bound, its upper bound or strictly
between, giving up to `3^n` patterns [derived].

`portopt` uses the dual active-set method of Goldfarb and Idnani (1983)
[cited: Goldfarb and Idnani 1983], the algorithm behind R's `quadprog`.
It starts from the unconstrained minimiser `w = 0` of `(1/2) w' Sigma w`,
adds the equalities, then repeatedly adds the most violated inequality,
taking the largest step that keeps every inequality multiplier
non-negative and dropping a constraint whose multiplier would turn
negative [derived: `_dual_active_set_qp` in `markowitz.py`]. Each step
increases the dual objective, so no active set repeats and the method is
finite [cited: Goldfarb and Idnani 1983]. Every linear solve uses the
pinned Cholesky of `Sigma` (factored once) or of the small Gram matrix
`N' Sigma^{-1} N` of the active constraint normals.

At the end the solver polishes: it re-solves the equality-constrained
problem on the final active set in closed form, applies one step of
iterative refinement, and sets every active bound exactly to its value
[derived]. The answer is then a function of the active set rather than
of the iteration path, which is why the four languages agree to about
1e-13 [measured: golden QP cases at tolerance 1e-10; COOKBOOK 12].

Two degenerate cases are handled explicitly [derived: `markowitz.py`]:
a fixed coordinate (`lb_i == ub_i`) enters as an equality, and the
maximum-return endpoint of the constrained frontier is reduced to a
sum-only QP over the assets tied at the greedy cut level.

**Glossary.** *Active set*: the constraints that hold with equality at
the solution. *Dual method*: it keeps the multipliers feasible and works
towards primal feasibility, the reverse of a primal method.

**Worked example** [measured: COOKBOOK 17]. The long-only GMV problem
takes 4 iterations, the box problem `0.02 <= w <= 0.40` takes 8, and the
long-only frontier point at 8% takes 9.

### 4.2 The KKT conditions and what the certificate checks

**Plain idea.** At the optimum, no small feasible move lowers the
variance. For a convex problem this is captured by a short list of
conditions on the weights and on one "price" per constraint (the
multiplier). If all of them hold, the point is the optimum.

**Formal statement.** Write every constraint as `n_c'w >= b_c` (lower
bound: `n_c = e_i`, `b_c = lb_i`; upper bound: `n_c = -e_i`,
`b_c = -ub_i`) or `a_j'w = b_j` (the budget row `1`, the target row `mu`,
fixed coordinates). The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for
`min (1/2) w' Sigma w` are [cited: Nocedal and Wright 2006, ch. 12 and 16]:

1. Stationarity: `Sigma w = sum_j lambda_j a_j + sum_c u_c n_c`.
2. Primal feasibility: `a_j'w = b_j` for all j, `n_c'w >= b_c` for all c.
3. Dual feasibility: `u_c >= 0` for every inequality.
4. Complementary slackness: `u_c (n_c'w - b_c) = 0` for every inequality.

For a strictly convex QP with linear constraints these conditions are
necessary and sufficient, so a point that satisfies all four is the
unique minimiser [cited: Nocedal and Wright 2006]. The equality
multipliers `lambda_j` have no sign condition.

What `QpResult.converged` checks in 1.2.0, read from `_kkt_certificate`
in `python/src/portopt/markowitz.py` [derived]:

* **Primal feasibility**, checked: the largest equality residual
  `|a_j'w - b_j|` and the largest inequality violation
  `max(0, b_c - n_c'w)` over all constraints must be at most
  `QP_CONV_TOL * (1 + max|b|)`, with `QP_CONV_TOL = 1e-9`.
* **Stationarity**, checked: `|Sigma w - sum_active u_i n_i|_inf` must be
  at most `1e-9 * (1 + |Sigma w|_inf + sum_i |u_i| |n_i|_inf)`. The
  multipliers `u` here are the polished ones from the final Gram solve,
  not the ones the loop carried.
* **Dual feasibility**, checked: every polished multiplier on an active
  inequality must satisfy `u_i >= -1e-9 * (1 + |Sigma w|_inf + sum_i
  |u_i| |n_i|_inf)`, the same scale as stationarity, since a multiplier
  has the units of the gradient.
* **Complementary slackness**, holds by construction rather than by a
  test: only active constraints carry a multiplier, and every active
  bound is set exactly to its bound before the check.

Before 1.2.0 the certificate tested only primal feasibility and
stationarity. The loop's step rule keeps the multipliers non-negative,
but the polished multipliers were never sign-checked, so a wrong active
set (for example after a rounding-level degenerate constraint is skipped
under `QP_DEGENERATE_TOL = 1e-9`) could have been certified. No failing
case was ever constructed; it was a gap in the proof, and the 1.2.0 check
closes it [cited: project review 2026-10-31, finding 5].

Two further details: the certificate is computed on the polished point
before the final clip of `w` to `[lb, ub]`, and `kkt_residual` reports
the largest of the feasibility, stationarity and dual-feasibility
violations (plus the target miss at the frontier's maximum-return
endpoint) [derived].

**Glossary.** *Multiplier*: the rate at which the optimal objective would
change if the constraint were relaxed by one unit. *Certificate*: a check
that proves optimality from the returned point alone, without trusting
the iteration that produced it.

**Worked example** [measured: COOKBOOK 17, inputs: annualised sample
covariance]. For the long-only GMV (`minvar_longonly_qp`), stationarity
reads `Sigma w = gamma * 1 + u` with `u_i >= 0` on the assets held at
zero. On the six assets with positive weight, `(Sigma w)_i =
0.0016283139`, so `gamma = 0.0016283139`. This equals the portfolio
variance (golden `minvar_longonly_qp.variance = 0.0016283139`) because
`w' Sigma w = gamma * 1'w + u'w = gamma` once complementary slackness
gives `u'w = 0` [derived]. The two zero-weight assets have multipliers
`u_EU_EQ = 1.03e-5` and `u_EM_EQ = 6.77e-4`, both positive, so dual
feasibility holds with a wide margin. In the box problem (`minvar_box_qp`) US_BOND
sits at its 0.40 cap; its upper-bound multiplier is
`gamma - (Sigma w)_US_BOND = 0.00203075 - 0.00137135 = 6.59e-4 >= 0`.

### 4.3 The exact box-simplex projection

**Plain idea.** To repair a weight vector that breaks the limits, we find
the nearest vector that respects them. The answer subtracts one constant
from every weight and clips.

**Formal statement.** The KKT conditions of `min |w - v|^2` over
`{sum w = total, lb <= w <= ub}` give `w_i = clip(v_i - t, lb_i, ub_i)`
for a single shift `t` [derived]. `g(t) = sum_i clip(v_i - t, lb_i,
ub_i)` is piecewise linear and non-increasing with kinks at `v_i - lb_i`
and `v_i - ub_i`, so sorting the breakpoints and interpolating on the
bracketing segment gives the exact `t` in finite time
[derived: `project_simplex_box`; cited: Duchi et al. 2008, Kiwiel 2008].

**Worked example** [measured: golden `simplex_projection_hand`]. Project
`[0.9, 0.5, -0.2]` onto the simplex. The third coordinate clips to 0, so
`(0.9 - t) + (0.5 - t) = 1`, giving `t = 0.2` and `w = [0.7, 0.3, 0.0]`.

### 4.4 Why practitioners want the constraint

**Plain idea.** Banning shorts removes the positions the optimiser takes
most often by mistake, so it acts like a filter on estimation noise.

**Formal statement.** Imposing `w >= 0` on minimum variance is equivalent
to solving the unconstrained problem on a modified covariance in which
the covariances of the would-be-shorted assets are shrunk
[cited: Jagannathan and Ma 2003]. Constraining weights is a form of
covariance shrinkage.

**Worked example** [measured: golden `minvar_unconstrained`,
`minvar_longonly_qp`]:

```
                      US_EQ   EU_EQ   JP_EQ   UK_EQ   EM_EQ  US_BOND IG_CORP FX_CARRY
minvar_unconstrained  0.055   0.009   0.022   0.032  -0.046   0.701   0.039   0.187
minvar_longonly_qp    0.037   0.000   0.016   0.021   0.000   0.732   0.015   0.179
```

The constrained solution zeroes the short (EM_EQ) and the small long
(EU_EQ). The constrained sweep runs from the constrained GMV return
(3.04% long-only versus 3.22% unconstrained) to the best single asset
(9.40%) [measured: demo]. Each constrained frontier point meets its
target as an equality `mu'w = m`; the demo reports a largest miss of
1.4e-17 [measured: demo].

### 4.5 A design note: why not projected gradient

An earlier version of this project solved the constrained frontier by
projected gradient descent on a quadratic penalty `(rho/2)(mu'w - m)^2`
[measured: recorded in the project history, not re-run here]. The
penalty made the Hessian badly conditioned (about 7e5), a
10,000-iteration cap returned a point about 1.7e-3 from the minimiser,
and a "max step < 1e-12" stopping rule certified nothing. The active-set
method has no penalty parameter and its stopping condition is the KKT
check of section 4.2.

## 5. Black-Litterman

### 5.1 Reverse optimisation: start from a defensible prior

**Plain idea.** Instead of estimating expected returns from noisy
history, ask which expected returns would make today's market weights
optimal. Use those as the starting point.

**Formal statement.** If the market portfolio `w_mkt` is optimal for an
investor with risk aversion `delta`, inverting `w* = (1/delta)
Sigma^{-1} mu` gives the implied equilibrium excess returns
[cited: Black and Litterman 1992]:

```
pi = delta * Sigma * w_mkt
```

Feeding `pi` back into tangency at `rf = 0` returns exactly `w_mkt`
[derived; measured: test suites]. Because `pi` is built from `Sigma`,
highly correlated assets receive similar implied returns, so the
optimiser has no spurious long-short bets to make [derived].

**Glossary.** *Excess return*: return above the risk-free rate.
*Reverse optimisation*: solving for the inputs that make a given
portfolio optimal.

**Worked example** [measured: golden `implied_pi`, delta = 2.5]. Equities
imply 2.8% to 3.9%; US_BOND implies 0.014%, because it has low volatility
and low covariance with the cap-weighted portfolio. The cap weights come
from `data/market_caps.csv`, which states arbitrary notional caps for all
eight assets, including the bond and FX proxies [assumed].

### 5.2 Views, tau, Omega

**Plain idea.** Add opinions ("EM_EQ will return 10%", "US_EQ will beat
EU_EQ by 2%") with a confidence each. The result is a weighted
compromise between equilibrium and opinion, and every asset adjusts
through its correlations.

**Formal statement.** Views are `P mu = Q + noise`, one row per view: an
absolute view is a unit row, a relative view is a +1/-1 row
[derived: `views_to_matrices`]. With prior `mu ~ N(pi, tau*Sigma)` and
view noise covariance `Omega`, Bayes gives the posterior mean. The
textbook form is

$$
\mu_{\text{post}}
= \left[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\right]^{-1}
  \left[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\pi + P^\top \Omega^{-1} Q\right],
$$

which is algebraically identical to the form the code pins, needing one
k x k Cholesky solve for k views [derived: `bl_posterior`]:

$$
\mu_{\text{post}}
= \pi + \tau\Sigma P^\top
  \left(P\,\tau\Sigma\,P^\top + \Omega\right)^{-1} (Q - P\pi).
$$

Read right to left: `Q - P*pi` is the surprise, how far the view is from
equilibrium; the middle matrix weights it by relative confidence;
`tau*Sigma*P'` spreads the update to every asset through the covariance.
The posterior covariance is `Sigma_post = Sigma + M` with
`M = tau*Sigma - tau*Sigma P' (P tau Sigma P' + Omega)^{-1} P tau*Sigma`,
the remaining uncertainty about the mean [derived].

The two scaling parameters:

* **tau** scales prior uncertainty about the mean relative to return
  volatility. A common convention is 0.01 to 0.1 (here 0.05), on the
  argument that a mean estimated from T observations has covariance
  about `Sigma/T` [assumed]. The code enforces `0 < tau <= 1`.
* **Omega** is the view noise covariance. `portopt` uses the He and
  Litterman (1999) default `Omega = diag(P (tau Sigma) P')`, each entry
  divided by a per-view confidence `c_k` [cited: He and Litterman 1999;
  derived: `default_omega`]. Large `c` makes the view nearly certain,
  small `c` makes it nearly ignored. This divisor is a scaling, not
  Idzorek's (2005) calibration, which solves for the Omega that delivers
  a stated fraction of the full-confidence tilt; that calibration is not
  implemented [cited: Idzorek 2005].

With the default Omega, both terms of the update scale with tau, so the
posterior mean does not depend on tau [derived]. Tau matters only when
Omega is supplied directly. The limit tests therefore fix Omega before
sending `tau -> 0` (posterior to pi) or `Omega -> infinity` (posterior to
pi, `M -> tau*Sigma`) [derived: `python/tests/test_black_litterman.py`].

**Glossary.** *Pick matrix P*: one row per view, saying which assets the
view is about. *Confidence c*: a divisor on the default view variance.

**Worked example** [measured: golden `bl_omega_default`]. With tau = 0.05
and confidences (1.0, 0.5), `Omega_00 = 2.0637e-3` and
`Omega_11 = 8.7083e-4`. Tau invariance: the EM_EQ posterior is
0.0693978137 for tau = 0.01, 0.05 and 0.5 [measured: COOKBOOK 16].

### 5.3 Worked posterior and its limits

**Worked example** [measured: golden `bl_posterior_returns`,
`bl_posterior_weights`]. Equilibrium says EM_EQ earns 3.85%, the view
says 10%, and the posterior is 6.94%. Other assets move too: US_EQ goes
from 3.26% to 5.31%, partly through correlation with EM_EQ and partly
from the relative view. The posterior tangency weights move from the
market: EM_EQ 5.8% to 28.2%, US_EQ 24.8% to 41.7%, EU_EQ 16.5% to
-10.2%, with gross leverage 1.20 [measured: demo].

Varying both confidences together moves the EM_EQ posterior from 3.91%
(c = 0.01) to 9.94% (c = 100) and its weight from 6.4% to 42.2%
[measured: COOKBOOK 16].

Three properties of the implementation matter in use [derived:
`bl_weights`; measured: COOKBOOK 16]:

* `bl_weights` returns the tangency portfolio of `(mu_post, Sigma_post)`
  at `rf = 0`, which is normalised to sum to 1. The unnormalised
  mean-variance weights `(delta Sigma_post)^{-1} mu_post` sum to 1.25 on
  the demo data, so the normalisation reduces the risk level by about a
  fifth.
* A sufficiently bearish view can make `A - rf*C <= 0` on the posterior,
  and `bl_weights` then raises `TangencyUndefinedError`. The demo's BL
  weight function does not catch it, so a rolling BL backtest with such
  views would stop [cited: project review 2026-10-31, finding 11].
* Conflicting extreme views keep `P tau Sigma P' + Omega` positive
  definite, so the posterior stays finite [derived; measured: test
  suites].

Black-Litterman does not fix `Sigma`, which is still estimated; delta and
tau are judgement; and the output is only as good as the views
[derived].

## 6. Risk parity and equal risk contribution

### 6.1 Risk contributions

**Plain idea.** Split the portfolio's risk into the part each asset is
responsible for. A portfolio can be balanced in money and badly
unbalanced in risk.

**Formal statement.** Variance is homogeneous of degree 2 in `w`, so
`sum_i w_i (Sigma w)_i = w' Sigma w` (Euler) and the normalised
contributions sum to 1 [derived]:

```
RC_i = w_i * (Sigma w)_i / (w' Sigma w),    sum_i RC_i = 1
```

Equal risk contribution (ERC) asks for `RC_i = 1/n`; risk budgeting asks
for `RC_i = b_i` with chosen budgets [cited: Maillard, Roncalli and
Teiletche 2010]. Neither uses expected returns.

**Glossary.** *Risk contribution*: an asset's share of portfolio
variance. *Risk budget*: the share we want it to have.

**Worked example.** A 60/40 capital split (12% in each of the five equity
lines, 13.3% in each of the three bond-like lines) puts 89.7% of the
variance in equities [measured: COOKBOOK 15]. Inverse-volatility
weighting still leaves US_BOND with 3.2% of the risk while each equity
contributes about 15% [measured: demo; golden `inverse_vol`]. Asking for
risk budgets of 60% equity and 40% bonds instead puts only 27.0% of
capital in equities [measured: COOKBOOK 15].

### 6.2 The CCD algorithm (pinned)

**Plain idea.** Adjust one weight at a time so that its risk share moves
towards its budget, cycle through the assets, and repeat until nothing
changes.

**Formal statement.** ERC weights have no closed form for general
`Sigma`. The pinned solver minimises Spinu's (2013) log-barrier objective
over `y > 0` [cited: Spinu 2013]:

```
F(y) = (1/2) y' Sigma y - sum_i b_i * ln(y_i)
```

At the stationary point `y_i (Sigma y)_i = b_i`, so `w = y / sum(y)` has
`RC_i = b_i` [derived]. Cyclical coordinate descent (CCD) updates one
coordinate at a time [cited: Griveau-Billion, Richard and Roncalli 2013].
Holding `m_i = (Sigma y)_i - Sigma_ii y_i` fixed, `dF/dy_i = 0` is a
quadratic with one positive root [derived]:

```
y_i = ( -m_i + sqrt(m_i^2 + 4*Sigma_ii*b_i) ) / (2*Sigma_ii)
```

`F` is strictly convex on the positive orthant, so the solution is unique
[derived]. The sweep order (ascending i), the incremental `Sigma y`
cache, the tolerance 1e-10, the start `y_i = 1/n` and the 10000-sweep cap
are pinned so that ports match [derived: `risk_parity.py`].

**Worked example** [measured: golden `erc_weights`, `risk_budget_pinned`;
COOKBOOK 9]. On the annualised sample covariance CCD stops after 14
sweeps with an RC spread (max RC minus min RC) of 1.3e-12. The ERC
portfolio holds 40.4% US_BOND and 17.6% IG_CORP. An asset with almost
zero volatility takes weight close to 1, which is the correct ERC limit
and a reason to cap weights in practice [derived; measured: test suites].

### 6.3 Convergence reporting

**Plain idea.** An iterative solver should tell the caller when it
stopped early, and a function that returns only weights should refuse to
return weights it does not trust.

**Formal statement.** `risk_budget_solve` stops when
`max_i |y_i - y_i_prev| < tol` after a full sweep, and returns
`(weights, sweeps, converged)` [derived: `risk_parity.py`]. Two
properties follow:

* **The tolerance is absolute.** Scaling `Sigma` by `s` scales the
  solution `y` by `1/sqrt(s)` (from `y_i (Sigma y)_i = b_i`), so the same
  `tol` is a different relative accuracy at different scales [derived].
  Measured on the bundled data: daily covariance, 16 sweeps and RC spread
  2.4e-14; annualised, 14 sweeps and 1.3e-12; annualised times 1e4, 12
  sweeps and 1.6e-10 [measured: COOKBOOK 9].
* **The weights-only wrappers raise.** Since 1.2.0 `risk_budget_weights`
  raises `ValueError` when `risk_budget_solve` reports
  `converged = False`, and `erc_weights` calls `risk_budget_weights`, so
  both behave like the QP wrappers [derived: `risk_parity.py`]. Before
  1.2.0 they discarded the flag: with `max_sweeps=2` the iterate has an
  RC spread of 1.0e-2 [measured: COOKBOOK 9, via `risk_budget_solve`],
  and `erc_weights` returned it without complaint [cited: project review
  2026-10-31, finding 4]. `risk_budget_solve` still returns the flag
  instead of raising, for callers who want to inspect the iterate. The
  stopping rule is still absolute; a relative rule is a possible
  follow-up.

**Glossary.** *Sweep*: one pass of updates over all n coordinates.

### 6.4 Leverage and the all-weather context

**Plain idea.** ERC loads up on low-volatility assets, so unlevered it
behaves like a bond portfolio. Risk-parity funds borrow to raise its
volatility, which is a separate bet.

ERC puts 40% in US_BOND and 17.6% in IG_CORP here, and the unlevered ERC
backtest has 5.2% volatility [measured: demo]. Risk-parity funds in the
"All Weather" style lever the portfolio to a higher target volatility,
implicitly betting that low-risk assets earn more per unit of risk than
the CAPM implies [cited: Frazzini and Pedersen 2014]. Leverage adds
financing cost, path dependence and forced deleveraging when
correlations rise; 2022, when US stocks and bonds both fell over the
calendar year, is the usual example [cited: widely reported index
returns, not computed here]. `portopt` implements only the unlevered
portfolio; leverage is a policy decision [derived].

## 7. The backtest, turnover and costs

### 7.1 The backtest as implemented

**Plain idea.** Pretend to run the strategy through history: at each
rebalance date use only the data available then, hold the resulting
weights until the next date, and record what the portfolio earned.

**Formal statement** [derived: `python/src/portopt/backtest.py`]:

* Rebalance dates are `t_k = W + 63k` for `k = 0, 1, ...` while
  `t_k < T`, with estimation window `W = 500`. With T = 1250 that gives
  12 dates, 500 to 1193, and a holdout of 750 days (2.98 years of 252
  days).
* At `t_k` the weight function sees the expanding history, rows
  `[0, t_k)`, as a read-only view. The weights apply to rows
  `[t_k, t_{k+1})`. No row at or after `t_k` is visible, so there is no
  look-ahead.
* Within a block the daily portfolio return is exactly `w_k' r_t`. That
  is a **daily-rebalanced constant mix**: the portfolio is assumed to
  trade back to the target weights every day, not to hold fixed share
  counts for a quarter. The "quarterly" schedule refers only to how often
  the weights are re-estimated.
* Turnover at rebalance `k >= 1` is `sum_i |w_k,i - w_{k-1},i|`, the L1
  distance between consecutive **target** vectors. The initial position
  is not charged, and the daily trades that maintain the constant mix
  are not counted.
* No transaction costs, financing costs or borrow fees are charged.
* Metrics use **arithmetic annualisation**: `ann_return = 252 *
  mean(r)`, `ann_vol = sqrt(252) * std(r, ddof=1)`, `sharpe =
  (ann_return - rf) / ann_vol`. Maximum drawdown is computed on the
  compounded wealth path `V_t = V_{t-1}(1 + r_t)`.
* Nothing applies a gross-leverage cap. The tangency strategy in the demo
  runs at gross leverage up to 93.6 [measured: COOKBOOK 18].

**Glossary.** *Holdout*: the period the strategy is evaluated on, after
the first estimation window. *Constant mix*: a portfolio rebalanced to
fixed proportions at every step. *Arithmetic annualisation*: scaling the
mean daily return by 252, which overstates the compound growth rate by
roughly half the variance [derived].

**Worked example** [measured: demo; golden `backtest_equal_weight`,
`backtest_erc`, `turnover_minvar_longonly`]:

| Method | Ann. return | Ann. vol | Sharpe | Max DD | Avg turnover |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| equal_weight | 6.14% | 9.30% | 0.445 | 18.7% | 0.000 |
| min_var_lo | 4.79% | 4.02% | 0.695 | 5.9% | 0.027 |
| tangency | 48.35% | 57.49% | 0.806 | 47.0% | 18.197 |
| black_litt | 7.01% | 10.70% | 0.469 | 23.3% | 0.017 |
| erc | 5.28% | 5.16% | 0.637 | 10.2% | 0.005 |
| inverse_vol | 5.64% | 6.30% | 0.578 | 12.4% | 0.004 |

On synthetic data, with no costs, and with the error bars of section
7.2, this table describes the code's behaviour. It is not evidence that
any method works.

### 7.2 How precise is a Sharpe ratio

**Plain idea.** A Sharpe ratio measured on three years of data is itself
a noisy estimate. Its error bar is wide enough to cover every number in
the table above.

**Formal statement.** For independent, identically distributed returns,
the per-period Sharpe estimate `SR_p` has asymptotic variance
`(1 + SR_p^2 / 2) / T` [cited: Lo 2002]. Annualising with `q` periods a
year multiplies the Sharpe ratio and its standard error by `sqrt(q)`:

```
SE(SR_ann) = sqrt(q) * sqrt( (1 + SR_p^2 / 2) / T ),   SR_p = SR_ann / sqrt(q)
```

Lo (2002) also gives the correction for serially correlated returns. The
synthetic returns are independent across days by construction
[derived: `generate_returns` in `data/generate_data.py`], so the iid
formula applies here; on real data it usually does not [cited: Lo 2002].

**Worked example** [derived, inputs measured: demo]. For `min_var_lo`,
`SR_ann = 0.6948`, `q = 252`, `T = 750`:

```
SR_p   = 0.6948 / sqrt(252)            = 0.04377
SE_p   = sqrt((1 + 0.04377^2/2) / 750) = 0.03653
SE_ann = sqrt(252) * 0.03653           = 0.580
95% interval: 0.695 +/- 1.96 * 0.580 = [-0.44, 1.83]
```

The standard error is 0.58 for every method in the table, because
`SR_p^2/2` is negligible next to 1 [measured: COOKBOOK 18]. Every 95%
interval contains zero, and the largest Sharpe gap in the table (0.36
between tangency and equal weight) is 0.6 standard errors of a single
estimate. The volatility and drawdown ordering, which follows from how
each method is built, is the part of the table worth reading [derived].

### 7.3 Transaction costs and turnover

**Plain idea.** Every trade costs money. A strategy that reshuffles its
whole book many times a quarter pays that cost many times, and a
backtest that ignores it overstates the result.

**Formal statement.** A linear cost model charges `c` per unit of
turnover, where one unit is notional equal to NAV traded. With turnover
`TO_k` at rebalance `k`, the cost in return terms is `c * TO_k` on that
day, and under arithmetic annualisation the annual drag is
[derived; assumed: linear costs, no market impact]

```
drag = c * (sum_k TO_k) / years
```

**Why tangency turns over 18x NAV per quarter.** The tangency weight
function falls back to unconstrained GMV whenever tangency is undefined.
At the first 8 dates the expanding-window GMV return `A/C` is below
rf = 2%, so the strategy holds GMV and turns over 0.025 to 0.063 per
quarter. At `t = 1004` `A/C` rises to 2.073%, just above rf; `A - rf*C`
is small, and the tangency weights have gross leverage 93.6. Switching
from GMV to that portfolio costs a turnover of 94.0. The next three
rebalances, at gross leverage 28.8, 42.0 and 15.1, add 64.7, 13.8 and
27.3 [measured: COOKBOOK 18]. The 11 turnovers sum to 200.2, and the mean
is 18.197 [measured: demo]. The 48% annual return in the table comes from
those last four quarters of extreme leverage.

**Worked example: 5 bp per unit of turnover** [derived from the measured
turnovers; assumed: c = 5 bp, linear]:

* Target-to-target turnover: `0.0005 * 200.2 / 2.976 = 3.36%` a year for
  tangency. For ERC (total 0.056) it is 0.001% a year.
* The daily trades that keep the constant mix are larger. After a day
  with returns `r`, weights drift to `w_i (1 + r_i) / (1 + w'r)`;
  restoring `w` trades `sum_i |w_i - w_i (1 + r_i)/(1 + w'r)|`. Summed
  over the holdout (excluding the rebalance days themselves), tangency
  trades 222x NAV a year, which at 5 bp is a further 11.1% a year. ERC
  trades 0.90x NAV a year (0.045% at 5 bp) and equal weight 1.09x
  (0.054%), although both report near-zero target turnover
  [measured: COOKBOOK 18].

Costs at this level change the ranking only for tangency; the cheaper
methods lose less than a tenth of a percent a year [derived]. Real costs
are not linear in size; a cost model with market impact grows faster
than linearly in trade size, which penalises the leveraged strategy more
[cited: Almgren and Chriss 2000]. The pinned backtest does not charge
costs, and no optimiser constrains turnover [derived].

**The research layer.** `python/src/portopt/research/` (Python only,
outside the cross-language contract) runs the same schedule with
buy-and-hold drift between rebalances, charges `cost_bps` on the traded
notional `sum_i |w_new_i - w_drift_i|` including the initial build, and
offers a no-trade band, partial rebalancing, volatility targeting and a
gross-leverage cap [derived: `research/costs.py`]. Its study,
[`docs/results/cost_aware_allocation.md`](docs/results/cost_aware_allocation.md),
reports for the tangency rule a turnover of 12.31x NAV per rebalance
after the initial build, a net Sharpe falling from 0.92 at 0 bp to 0.69
at 25 bp, and a cost of 11.8% of NAV a year at 25 bp; capping gross
leverage at 2 cuts turnover to 0.32 per rebalance; and no difference
between rules is statistically meaningful (largest DSR 0.58, smallest
paired p-value against equal weight 0.15 before correction)
[measured: `python python/studies/cost_aware_allocation.py`, synthetic
data]. These numbers differ from the ones above because the accounting
differs: drifted holdings between rebalances instead of a daily constant
mix, and a charged initial build.

### 7.4 Failure handling is part of the method

The pinned tangency policy falls back to unconstrained min-variance on
`TangencyUndefinedError` only and reports how often it did so; any other
error, such as a singular covariance, stops the run [derived:
`python/demo.py::tangency_fn`; API_SPEC 6.3]. The conditioning guard of
section 1.3 rejects tangency when rf is within one part per million of
the GMV return. At `t = 1004` rf was 7.3e-4 below `A/C`, outside the
guard, which is why that rebalance produced gross leverage 93.6
[measured: COOKBOOK 18].

## 8. Practical workflow and limits

### 8.1 How a desk would use this library

**Plain idea.** The library is a set of verified building blocks. A desk
supplies clean data, chooses the blocks, checks the outputs, and decides
what happens on every error.

1. **Assemble the panel.** A complete `T x N` matrix of daily simple
   returns (the library rejects NaN, so fill or drop upstream), a cap
   weight vector for the reverse-optimisation prior, and a view list.
   Keep `T >= N + 1` unless shrinkage is meant to repair a singular sample
   covariance [derived].
2. **Estimate.** `sample_mean`, `shrink_covariance` on daily returns,
   `annualize_*` with the desk's convention (pass `periods` explicitly).
   An intensity near 1 says the sample covariance carries little
   information at this `N/T` [derived].
3. **Build candidates.** The constrained QP (`min_variance_longonly_solve`
   with the mandate's bounds; `constrained_frontier_solve` at a target) is
   the main tool: it meets the target and the bounds exactly and returns
   a certificate. Check feasibility first with `attainable_return_range`.
   Treat unconstrained tangency and Black-Litterman weights as
   diagnostics unless `gross_leverage` is capped [derived].
4. **Check before anything reaches an order system.** `converged` true
   and `kkt_residual` small (COOKBOOK 17 repeats the check by hand);
   `risk_budget_solve` when the sweep count matters (section 6.3);
   `risk_contributions` for concentration; turnover against the previous
   target vector times the desk's cost per unit [derived].
5. **Backtest with error bars.** `run_backtest` reports Sharpe, drawdown
   and turnover. Subtract costs yourself (section 7.3) and attach the
   standard error of section 7.2 [derived].

Market conventions, since the universe mixes asset classes:

* 252 trading days is the equity convention used throughout. FX and
  rates desks often use 260 or 365 for carry; the choice must be pinned
  [assumed].
* Returns are daily simple returns, and the portfolio return is the
  weighted sum, which is exact under daily rebalancing to target. Log
  returns aggregate over time instead; mixing the two biases long-horizon
  numbers [derived].
* `FX_CARRY` stands for a funded excess-return series; equity index
  returns are total returns, from which rf is subtracted for Sharpe.
  Black-Litterman works in excess-return space throughout, which is why
  `bl_weights` pins `rf = 0` [derived; assumed: labelling of the
  synthetic series].

Common numerical pitfalls and how the code handles them [derived]:

1. **Singular covariance** (duplicated asset, T < N): the Cholesky pivot
   test `s <= 1e-13 * a_ii` raises a clear error.
2. **Near-singular covariance**: frontier and tangency weights become
   extreme long before Cholesky fails. Inspect `D`, the condition number
   (246 for the annualised sample covariance here [measured: numpy
   `cond`]) and the gross leverage, or shrink.
3. **Asymmetric inputs**: the gate `max|A - A'| <= 1e-10 (1 + max|A|)`
   rejects them. It accepts about 1e-6 relative asymmetry on daily
   covariances, and Cholesky reads only the lower triangle, so symmetrise
   inputs yourself [cited: project review 2026-10-31, finding 14].
4. **Explicit inversion**: every `Sigma^{-1} x` is a Cholesky solve.
5. **Annualise-then-optimise**: closed-form weights are invariant to
   scaling `Sigma` alone but not to scaling `(mu, Sigma)` with nonzero rf;
   the golden cases pin annualised inputs.
6. **Comparing iterative results across languages**: a truncated
   iteration depends on the floating-point path; a finite method with a
   closed-form polish, or a contractive iteration with a tight tolerance,
   depends on the answer, so agreement becomes a rounding question.
7. **Discarded convergence flags**: the QP and risk-parity wrappers
   raise on a failed certificate or an unconverged CCD (section 6.3); the
   `*_solve` variants return the flag.

### 8.2 From this library to a real desk

**Plain idea.** Everything in this repository is the allocation step on
clean synthetic inputs. A production process needs data, costs,
constraints, risk models and an evaluation protocol around it. The items
below are not in the pinned library; where the Python research layer
covers part of one, we say so [derived].

* **Real data.** Prices with corporate actions, holiday calendars,
  missing data and survivorship handled, plus a holdout frozen before any
  development. Not implemented.
* **Costs inside the loop.** Spread plus market impact per asset class,
  charged on drifted holdings, and turnover penalties or limits inside
  the optimiser rather than reported afterwards [cited: Almgren and
  Chriss 2000]. The Python research layer charges a linear cost on
  drifted holdings (section 7.3); market impact and turnover-aware
  optimisation are not implemented.
* **Constraints.** Gross and net leverage, sector and country limits,
  tracking error to a benchmark, minimum trade sizes. Only box bounds and
  the budget and target equalities exist here. Not implemented.
* **Factor risk models.** At hundreds of assets, desks use a factor
  covariance (exposures times factor covariance plus specific risk)
  rather than a sample or single-target shrinkage estimate
  [cited: Grinold and Kahn 2000]. Not implemented.
* **Out-of-sample protocol.** Walk-forward evaluation with purged splits,
  Sharpe standard errors, the deflated Sharpe ratio to account for the
  number of strategies tried [cited: Bailey and Lopez de Prado 2014], and
  bootstrap intervals. The Python research layer computes Lo (2002) and
  Mertens (2002) standard errors, probabilistic and deflated Sharpe
  ratios, a stationary block bootstrap and a paired Sharpe-difference
  test (`research/stats.py`); purged walk-forward splits and a frozen
  real-data holdout are not implemented.
* **The 1/N benchmark.** DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) found that
  none of 14 sample-based models consistently beat equal weighting out
  of sample, and estimated that sample mean-variance needs on the order
  of 3000 months of data to do so with 25 assets [cited: DeMiguel et al.
  2009]. Any claim of improvement should be stated against 1/N and
  inverse volatility, after costs. The demo reports equal weight but does
  not test against it; the research study runs a paired test against
  equal weight (section 7.3).

The realistic aim of a shrinkage-plus-constraints process is lower risk
and lower turnover than the naive alternatives, not alpha
[cited: DeMiguel et al. 2009; Jagannathan and Ma 2003].

### 8.3 Limitations scorecard

| Method | What it assumes | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Unconstrained Markowitz | mu and Sigma known; quadratic utility | Error maximisation (sec. 2); extreme, unstable, costly weights |
| GMV | Sigma known | Ignores mu by construction, but concentrates in low-vol assets and inherits Sigma noise |
| Long-only QP | Convexity; meaningful bounds | Corner solutions; implicit shrinkage may remove true hedges; dense solver, slow above about 50 assets |
| Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage | Constant correlation is a sensible prior | Biases pairs with unusual correlation; does nothing for mu; assumes iid returns |
| Black-Litterman | Market near equilibrium; calibrated views | delta, tau, Omega are judgement; cap prior ill-defined for bonds and FX; weights renormalised to sum 1 |
| ERC / risk budgeting | Risk is covariance; budgets meaningful | Ignores mu; unlevered version is bond-heavy; absolute stopping rule (sec. 6.3) |
| Backtest | Daily rebalance to target; no costs | Drift turnover not counted; costs not charged; one 750-day holdout gives Sharpe SE 0.58; synthetic data |

Limits shared by all of the above [derived]: variance treats upside and
downside alike; correlations change over time; and every method here is
single-period, so drawdown control, liabilities and tax need other
machinery.

## 9. Knowledge check: questions and worked answers

**Q1. Derive the minimum-variance portfolio and explain why it does not
depend on expected returns.**
Minimise `(1/2) w' Sigma w` subject to `1'w = 1`. Stationarity gives
`w = gamma Sigma^{-1} 1`, and the constraint gives `gamma = 1/C`, so
`w_mv = Sigma^{-1} 1 / (1' Sigma^{-1} 1)`. `mu` never enters because the
objective is variance alone, which is why GMV is the most stable point on
the frontier: it does not use the noisiest input.

**Q2. What is the two-fund theorem and why does it matter
computationally?**
Every frontier portfolio is `w(m) = g + h*m`, an affine family spanned by
two fixed portfolios. One Cholesky factorisation and two solves
(`Sigma^{-1} mu`, `Sigma^{-1} 1`) give the whole frontier;
`efficient_frontier` computes `g` and `h` once and each of the 25 points
is one vector update.

**Q3. Your optimiser says short EU_EQ 58% and hold US_EQ at 136%. What do
you tell the portfolio manager?**
That this is estimation error. The two series have sample correlation
0.82, so `Sigma^{-1}` turns a small and statistically insignificant
difference in sample means into a large relative-value position. On this
synthetic data we can check: the true drifts are 7% and 6%. Fixes:
shrink or structure the inputs (Ledoit-Wolf, Black-Litterman), constrain
the weights (long-only removes the trade), or drop mu (ERC). Also look at
turnover: a position that flips every rebalance is following noise.

**Q4. Why does the long-only constraint improve out-of-sample results if
it can only hurt the in-sample optimum?**
Jagannathan and Ma: the non-negativity constraint is equivalent to
shrinking the covariances of the would-be-shorted assets. In sample the
feasible set is smaller, so measured variance rises; out of sample the
removed positions were mostly noise, so realised risk falls.

**Q5. In Black-Litterman, what are tau and Omega, and what happens in the
limits?**
`tau*Sigma` is the covariance of our uncertainty about the mean; `Omega`
is the view noise covariance. With Omega fixed, `tau -> 0` makes the
prior dominate and the posterior tends to pi. `Omega -> infinity` ignores
the views: the posterior mean tends to pi and `M -> tau*Sigma`. With the
He-Litterman default `Omega = diag(P tau Sigma P')/c`, tau cancels from
the posterior mean and only the confidences matter.

**Q6. Why does BL use rf = 0 when computing posterior weights?**
`pi = delta Sigma w_mkt` are excess returns, and the posterior blends pi
with views, so `mu_post` is an excess return too. Subtracting rf again
would count the risk-free rate twice. With no views, tangency at rf = 0
on `(pi, Sigma)` returns the market weights exactly.

**Q7. Show that risk contributions sum to portfolio variance, and state
the ERC fixed-point condition.**
`sum_i w_i (Sigma w)_i = w' Sigma w` by Euler's theorem for the
degree-2 homogeneous variance, so normalised contributions sum to 1. The
CCD stationarity condition is `y_i (Sigma y)_i = b_i`; after normalising
`w = y / sum(y)`, `RC_i = b_i`. With equal budgets every asset
contributes the same variance (golden RC spread 1.3e-12).

**Q8. Risk parity did well for decades. Give the case for and against
attributing that to the method.**
For: balanced risk exposure collects the term premium, and low-beta
assets have historically earned more per unit of risk than the CAPM line
(Frazzini and Pedersen), so levering bonds was rewarded. Against: the
sample coincides with a long bond rally and falling financing costs; when
stock-bond correlation turned positive in 2022, diversification and
leverage hurt together. The defensible claim is risk balance, not a
return forecast.

**Q9. Your tangency call raises "tangency undefined". What happened and
what do you do?**
`A - rf*C <= 0`: rf is at or above the GMV return, so no line from
`(0, rf)` touches the upper frontier. With estimated means this is common
(8 of 12 rebalances in the bundled backtest). The same error fires when
rf is within a relative 1e-6 of the GMV return, where `A - rf*C` cancels
and the weights have gross leverage in the millions. Options: fall back
to GMV (the pinned choice), use excess-return Black-Litterman inputs, or
treat it as a sign that the estimated frontier is too flat to bet on.
Look at `gross_leverage` before trusting any unconstrained weights.

**Q10. Why does this project pin algorithms (Cholesky variant, sweep
order, active-set rules) rather than only tolerances?**
Different correct algorithms round differently, and truncated
iterations amplify path differences: a QP solver stopped by a step-size
rule can sit anywhere within its tolerance of the minimiser. Pinning the
operation sequence, and finishing the QP with a closed-form polish on the
identified active set, makes cross-language agreement a rounding
question (1e-10 to 1e-13). That is what makes `golden.json` enforceable
in four languages, and the suites check that scaling the input by one
ulp moves the QP goldens by less than 1e-13.

**Q11. List the KKT conditions for the long-only minimum-variance QP and
say which ones `QpResult.converged` verifies.**
Stationarity `Sigma w = gamma 1 + u`; primal feasibility `1'w = 1`,
`w >= 0`; dual feasibility `u >= 0`; complementary slackness
`u_i w_i = 0`. The certificate tests primal feasibility and stationarity
and, since 1.2.0, dual feasibility, all at 1e-9 relative. Complementary
slackness holds by construction because only active bounds carry
multipliers and they are set exactly to the bound (section 4.2).

**Q12. In the long-only GMV solution, why does the multiplier on the
budget constraint equal the portfolio variance?**
Multiply stationarity by `w'`: `w' Sigma w = gamma 1'w + u'w`. The budget
gives `1'w = 1` and complementary slackness gives `u'w = 0`, so
`w' Sigma w = gamma`. On the bundled data both are 0.0016283139
(golden `minvar_longonly_qp.variance`; COOKBOOK 17).

**Q13. What is the standard error of an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.69
measured on 750 daily returns, and what does it tell you?**
Lo (2002), iid case: `SR_p = 0.69 / sqrt(252) = 0.0437`,
`SE_p = sqrt((1 + SR_p^2/2)/750) = 0.0365`, `SE_ann = sqrt(252) * 0.0365
= 0.58`. The 95% interval runs from about -0.44 to 1.83, so the estimate
cannot distinguish the strategy from zero, let alone from another
strategy with Sharpe 0.6.

**Q14. The demo's tangency strategy turns over 18.2x NAV per quarter.
Where does that number come from, and what would 5 bp per unit cost?**
It is the mean of 11 target-to-target turnovers. Seven are below 0.07
(GMV fallback to GMV fallback); the switch from GMV to a tangency
portfolio with gross leverage 93.6 at `t = 1004` costs 94.0, and the
next three add 106. At 5 bp the 200.2 units cost 10.0% of NAV over 2.98
years, 3.36% a year, before the 11.1% a year from the daily constant-mix
trades that the backtest does not count.

**Q15. Equal weight reports zero turnover. Is it free to run?**
No. The backtest holds a daily-rebalanced constant mix, and target
turnover only measures changes between target vectors, which are zero
for 1/N. Restoring 1/N every day after prices move trades about 1.09x
NAV a year on the bundled data (COOKBOOK 18). A buy-and-hold variant
would avoid those trades but would no longer be equal weight.

**Q16. Why does the same CCD tolerance give an RC spread of 2.4e-14 on
the daily covariance but 1.6e-10 on the annualised covariance times
1e4?**
The stopping rule `max |y - y_prev| < 1e-10` is absolute in `y`, and
`y_i (Sigma y)_i = b_i` makes `y` scale like `1/sqrt(scale of Sigma)`.
A larger `Sigma` gives a smaller `y`, so the same absolute tolerance is a
looser relative tolerance. A relative stopping rule would remove the
dependence (section 6.3).

**Q17. Why does `delta` (the Ledoit-Wolf intensity) fall from 0.44 at
T = 10 to 0.007 at T = 1250 on the same assets?**
The intensity is roughly `(pi_hat - rho_hat) / (T * gamma_hat)`. The
numerator measures the sampling noise of the sample covariance entries
and the denominator the distance to the target; noise per entry falls
like `1/T` while the distance settles to a fixed value, so the optimal
weight on the target falls roughly like `1/T`.

**Q18. Black-Litterman with default Omega: you double tau. What happens
to the posterior mean, the posterior covariance and the weights?**
The posterior mean is unchanged, because `Omega` scales with tau and the
update `tau Sigma P'(P tau Sigma P' + Omega)^{-1}` is invariant
(measured: EM_EQ posterior identical for tau = 0.01, 0.05, 0.5). The
posterior covariance `Sigma + M` changes, because `M` scales with tau.
The weights therefore change slightly, through `Sigma_post`, even though
the mean does not.
